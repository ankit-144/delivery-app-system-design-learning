# 8.4 — Load shedding: keep checkout alive during a flash sale

> **Phase 8: operating it.** When demand exceeds capacity, something will fail. Shedding decides
> *what* fails, and makes it fail fast, instead of letting everything slow down until everything fails.

## Story

A flash sale. Checkouts run at a steady 40/s. At t=15 s, for 45 seconds, 300 extra requests/s browse the
catalogue, refresh best-sellers and check order history. No caches (the cache cluster was "being upgraded"), and
the naive order-history query (2.5). It's far more than three core instances and one Postgres can serve.

## Run it

```bash
make up PHASE=3                 # or any later phase
make load ID=8.4 MODE=broken    # load-shedding=off
make load ID=8.4 MODE=fixed     # load-shedding=priority, shed-browse-limit=4
```

| | Checkouts sent | Checkout success | Checkout p50 / p99 | Browsing served | Browsing refused fast (503) | Requests k6 couldn't even send |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| no shedding | 2,216 of 3,000 | **41.7%** | **30 s / 30 s** (client timeout) | 43% of 6,337, at a 30 s p50 | — | **7,827** |
| priority shedding | 3,001 | **100%** | **138 ms / 1,062 ms** | 1,419, p50 84 ms | **12,084**, in ≤ 3 ms | 0 |

Without shedding, the 123 "503"s weren't shedding at all: they were connection-pool timeouts, after ~16 s of waiting.
k6 ran out of virtual users because every request was stuck for 30 s: in real life those are customers staring at a
spinner, and many of them pressing refresh.

## What happened without shedding

Browsing and checkout compete for the same things: Tomcat threads, the 10 connections in each instance's pool, and
Postgres CPU. Best-sellers queries take 150–300 ms of Postgres time each. During the surge, every pool is full of
browsing queries, and a checkout that needs a connection waits in line behind them. It doesn't matter that checkout
is a 2 ms query: its wait is everything in front of it. Requests time out after 30 s. A customer who waited 30 s
for an error has cost the system exactly as much as one who got an answer.

## Priority shedding

[`LoadShedder`](../../services/core-service/src/main/java/dev/quickdrop/core/web/LoadShedder.java), a servlet filter
that runs before the request touches a thread pool's worth of work:

```
 request ──▶ sheddable? (GET product lists, best-sellers, order history)
               no  ──▶ always admitted (checkout, OTP, order tracking)
               yes ──▶ fewer than 4 sheddable requests in flight on this instance?
                         yes ──▶ admitted
                         no  ──▶ 503 + Retry-After: 2, in ~1 ms
```

With at most 4 browsing requests per instance holding connections, 6 of the 10 are always free for checkout.
Customers who were shed get an instant "busy, try again" rather than a 30-second spinner, and the app can show a
friendly message or retry after `Retry-After`.

## Designing it for real

- **Classify by business value**, not by URL alone: checkout, payment, login and order tracking first;
  recommendations, browsing and search second; analytics and exports last.
- **Shed early and cheaply**: at the load balancer or API gateway if possible, before the request costs anything.
- **Adaptive limits**: a fixed limit (4) is tuned for one machine and one workload. Netflix's
  `concurrency-limits` and Envoy's adaptive concurrency adjust the limit from observed latency (TCP-style
  congestion control for requests).
- **Queue time as a signal**: a request that waited longer than its client will wait is already wasted. Drop it
  (CoDel-style) instead of processing it.
- **Clients must cooperate**: honour `Retry-After`, back off with jitter (8.3). Otherwise shedding just invites an
  immediate retry.
- **Degrade, don't fail**: serve a cached or static best-sellers list (3.8's stale fallback) instead of a 503 when
  you can.
