# 6.4 — Postgres as the notification queue, during a blast

> **Phase 6: notifications, from a sync call to Kafka.** A durable queue on the database you already
> have. It works, until it competes with the work that matters.

## Story

Flash sale. Marketing blasts "Everything 50% off!" to every customer: **60,000 promos**, straight into
the notification queue table on the Postgres that also takes orders. Meanwhile customers order and open
"My orders".

## Run it

```bash
make load ID=6.4                       # 60,000 promos, Postgres with its normal 2 CPUs
make load ID=6.4 BLAST=240000 WORKERS=32
PG_CPUS=0.5 make up && make load ID=6.4 BLAST=120000     # production-like proportions (see below)
```

Run 6.5 right after each one: it's the same blast through Kafka.

## What we measured (and the surprise)

With Postgres on its 2 CPUs:

| | Postgres CPU peak | Orders p95 | "My orders" p95 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60k promos via the **Postgres queue** | 0.71 cores | 39 ms | 12 ms |
| 60k promos via **Kafka** (6.5) | 0.32 cores | 10 ms | 4 ms |
| 240k promos, 32 workers, Postgres queue | 0.74 cores | 14 ms | 4 ms |

**We expected the database queue to fall over, and it didn't.** 240,000 notifications, each inserted, claimed and
updated, cost Postgres under one CPU. `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` makes Postgres a genuinely good job queue, and
plenty of production systems run on one (pg-boss, river, good_job, Que). The honest lesson isn't "never queue in
Postgres".

The cost is real, though: every notification is **three writes on your most important database** (2× the CPU of
the Kafka run, 4× the order p95), and it scales with marketing's ambitions, not with your orders.

## At production proportions

Our laptop's Postgres is oversized for laptop traffic. Shrink it to half a CPU (`PG_CPUS=0.5`) so the
ratio of database capacity to traffic looks more like production, and blast 120,000:

| Postgres at 0.5 CPU | Postgres CPU | Orders p95 | Orders p99 | Backlog at the end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Postgres queue | **pinned at 100%** | **149 ms** | **500 ms** | 41,043 rows still PENDING |
| Kafka (6.5) | 58% | 39 ms | 56 ms | lag 110,509 → drained to 0 |

Now the blast and checkout fight for the same CPU, and checkout loses: p99 is 10× worse, during the most
important hour of the year. A bigger spike or a smaller database, and orders start timing out.

## What a Postgres queue costs

- **It shares fate with your core data**: CPU, connections, IOPS, locks, backups, failover.
- **Write amplification**: insert + claim (update) + done (update), plus index maintenance, plus WAL that replicas
  must replay (5.1).
- **Dead rows**: every update leaves one behind (4.5). A high-churn queue table needs aggressive autovacuum.
- **Polling**: idle workers ask "anything new?" every 50 ms. That's 32 workers × 20 queries/s ≈ 640 queries/s of
  pure overhead (**Postgres → Top queries by calls**). `LISTEN/NOTIFY` can wake workers instead.
- **One consumer per message**: no fan-out to analytics or other services (K9), and no replay (K7).

## When it's the right tool

Moderate volume (hundreds to a few thousand jobs per second), jobs that must commit **atomically with your data**
(enqueue in the same transaction, the outbox idea of 6.6), and teams that don't want to run Kafka. Use a separate
database for the queue once it starts competing with checkout.
