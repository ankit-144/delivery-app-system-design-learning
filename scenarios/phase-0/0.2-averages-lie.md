# 0.2 — Averages lie

> **Phase 0: baseline.** Before measuring anything, learn which numbers to trust.

## Story

The order confirmation SMS is sent inline. The vendor usually answers in ~30 ms, but **2% of calls
take 1.5 s**: a retry inside their network, a slow carrier gateway. Every real dependency has a tail
like this. The weekly report says "average order latency: 71 ms". Product is happy.

## Run it

```bash
make load ID=0.2               # vendor with a 2% slow tail
make load ID=0.2 MODE=fixed    # same vendor, no tail
```

What we measured:

```
  average      71.3 ms
  p50          39.8 ms
  p95          44.8 ms
  p99        1504.7 ms
  max        1507.3 ms
```

## Observe

**Overview → Order placement latency** has four lines: p50, p95, p99 and the average.

- The **average** sits between p50 and p99, describing **nobody's** experience. No customer waited
  71 ms. 98% waited ~40 ms and 2% waited 1.5 s.
- **p99** is the line that tells the truth about the tail.

## Why percentiles

A percentile answers a customer question: *"p99 = 1.5 s"* means 1 in 100 requests took at least 1.5 s.

Why the tail matters more than it looks:

- **Heavy users hit it most.** A customer who opens the app 20 times a day and makes 5 requests each
  time makes 100 requests a day, so they hit the p99 about once a day, every day.
- **Fan-out multiplies it.** A page that makes 10 backend calls in parallel is as slow as its
  slowest call. With a 1% tail per call, about 10% of page loads hit at least one slow call.
- **Averages are dragged by outliers in both directions.** A few very fast cache hits make the
  average look better while the tail gets worse.

## How we measure percentiles

Spring Boot records each request in a **histogram**: counters for "≤ 1 ms", "≤ 5 ms", "≤ 10 ms"… Prometheus
computes percentiles from those buckets:

```promql
histogram_quantile(0.99, sum by (le) (rate(quickdrop_order_placement_seconds_bucket[1m])))
```

Two consequences worth knowing:

1. **You can add histograms across instances** (sum the buckets, then compute the quantile). You can't
   average percentiles: the average of three instances' p99 is not the p99 of all traffic.
2. **Buckets limit precision.** A p99 of "1.5 s" means "somewhere in the bucket that contains 1.5 s".

## Try

- `make load ID=0.2 SLOW_PERCENT=10`: now p95 jumps too. Which percentile you watch decides which
  problems you can see.
- Look at **Overview → k6 request duration (client side)** next to the server-side latency. The
  client always sees a bit more: nginx, the network, queueing before Tomcat even starts the timer.
